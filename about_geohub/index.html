GeoHUB

A hazard GIS on a 3D globe. Free, in your browser, with nothing to install.

Open the globe and the whole hazard record is already on it — 312,500 earthquakes back to 1008, every cyclone track since 1842, world geology, live events, live satellites, and ten worlds beyond Earth. Draw an area, sample every layer under it, take the table away. Then mesh it, model it, and write it up in the same window. Membership adds the six hazard models GeoID computes itself.

Why GeoHUB

Geoscience Without Gatekeepers

The data that describes our hazards is public. Getting at it is not: the catalogues are scattered across a dozen agencies in a dozen formats, and the software that reads them is a licensed desktop install with a learning curve to match.

GeoHUB collapses that into a URL. The catalogues arrive already merged, already symbolised and already on a globe you can spin — and everything behind them stays open, so you can switch a layer on, recolour it, click into it and export it rather than take our word for what it says. The globe, the records and the tools are free with no account. The hazard maps GeoID computes on top of them are what a membership buys.

The record

Three Centuries of Hazard, on a Timeline

Not a susceptibility picture — the actual events, merged from the source catalogues and played back frame by frame.

  • 312,500 earthquakes, magnitude 4.5 and up, from the year 1008 to today — USGS ComCat, ISC-GEM and GEM GHEC merged into one record.
  • 13,513 tropical cyclones, every track on file from 1842 to 2026, coloured by peak wind.
  • Filter by magnitude or basin, then scrub the clock: 63 frames by year, or season by season.
  • Every layer keeps its licence and its DOI, so anything you derive from it inherits the same terms.

Study areas

Draw a Study Area, Pull the Data Inside It

A study area is the question every dataset on the globe is asked. Draw a polygon and GeoHUB fetches what lies inside it — live weather, Earth Engine archives and time series — instead of a picture of the whole world.

  • Draw a polygon, box or circle anywhere, or use any polygon layer as the study area — your own shapefile included. GeoHUB reports its true area.
  • Fetch the latest weather maps inside it: the RainViewer radar composite, and GFS and ICON precipitation, temperature and wind fields.
  • Pull Earth Engine datasets over it — rainfall, land surface temperature, vegetation, soil moisture, imagery — as a single map or a time-lapse across a range of dates.
  • Record time series at stations inside it: forecast rainfall and every term of the slope and flood models, plotted and exported as CSV.
  • Clip geology, elevation and every other active layer to it, exactly, as CSV, GeoJSON or shapefile, or take the terrain under it into the Meshing Studio.

Meshing Studio

From Terrain to Finite Elements

A GIS tells you what is there. A model tells you what it will do. The studio is the bridge, and it runs in the same tab.

  • Pull the real terrain under your study area and build it into a solid — surface, subsurface and atmosphere as named volumes.
  • Boolean geometry, physical groups, structured or unstructured meshing, and a clip plane to see inside.
  • Assign materials and boundary conditions, then hand the mesh to a finite-element solve.
  • The browser configures the run; the solver runs where it can — locally, or on a machine you point it at.
What else is on the globe

A Multidisciplinary Workspace

Seismology, volcanology, geology, rock mechanics, storms, orbits and the planet's interior, side by side on one globe. Every one is shipped, live and one click from the layer catalogue — no account, no install, no quota.

Live events, as they are reported

Wildfires, storms, floods, volcanic activity and earthquakes streaming from NASA EONET and USGS — browsed by category or as a live feed, each one clickable through to the source record.

Seismograms in the popup

Click a quake and GeoHUB fetches the nearest station's waveform, draws the trace and its spectrogram, and marks the modelled P and S arrivals — then saves it to your project.

World geology, clickable

Macrostrat's global map streams as vector tiles. Click any unit for its age, lithology, area and rock properties — with coastlines, rivers, boundaries, plate boundaries and active faults shipped alongside.

Rock strength from the map

Geological Strength Index derived from mapped lithology, banded into typical field ranges — an engineering property painted straight onto the geology, with the unmapped cases named rather than guessed.

Volcanic hazard buffers

Every volcano in the Smithsonian GVP catalogue, ringed by graduated hazard zones from ballistics and pyroclastic flows at the vent out to trace ash at 50 km — each with its rock type, region and eruptive history.

Every storm that ever tracked

The full IBTrACS archive drawn at once, coloured by peak wind — or played back season by season to watch basins fire in turn.

What's overhead right now

Space stations, science, weather, navigation and geostationary satellites propagated from CelesTrak elements, drawn at true altitude and refreshed every 1.5 seconds.

Cut the planet open

Core View slices the globe to crust, mantle, outer core and inner core — and every layer you have switched on is cut with it, so your data goes down the wall of the cut.

How it fits together

One Workspace, Four Modes

GeoHUB is not four apps behind one menu. It is one window with one project, and the three mode buttons in the header — GIS, Model, Research — change what you are doing to the same ground, so a study area drawn in the GIS is the mesh in the studio and the figure in the write-up, without an export in between. The ten worlds open in the same window, with the same tools.

GIS

The globe everything else runs on. The shipped catalogue and your own data on one sphere, with drawing, measurement, elevation profiles, sampling and export — and a legend that tells you where every number came from.

Explorers

Ten worlds in the same window with the same tools on each: distance and elevation profiles, named-feature search, geology and palaeo sea-level overlays, a core cutaway. Mars adds the flight simulator over the CTX mosaic. Every world, below.

Mesh Studio

Turn a study area into a mesh — pull the terrain, build the volume, set materials and boundary conditions, and hand it to a finite-element solve. The studio is free; the Model Builder, which samples the real ground into the domain, is part of membership.

Research Hub

A project is a real folder on disk — ingest, analysis, figures, notes and provenance — in the same layout our desktop application reads, so work moves between the two without export. Signal processing, statistics and event correlation run in the page; a GALES solve runs on your machine or a server you point it at.

Ten worlds

Mercury to Pluto, on Mission Data

The same window, the same tools, a different world: every viewer is built from the datasets its missions returned, and every one has distance and elevation profiles, named-feature search with a description behind each label, geology overlays and a core cutaway.

  • Mars is a flight simulator as well as a map: pick a launch site and heading anywhere on the planet and fly MOLA relief with the CTX mosaic streamed beneath you, from an 8 km low pass to 300 km orbit, shuttle or saucer, chase or cockpit view.
  • Twenty-four moons carry clickable surface features — 2,087 on the Moon alone — and each planet's moon system opens in its own orbital view.
  • Palaeo sea level on the rocky worlds: raise or lower a simulated ocean in real time and trace the coastlines scientists still argue over.
  • Free, no account, and reached from the GeoHUB header or the cards below.

Six Ready-to-Use Hazard Models, Computed in the Same Platform

The reason GeoHUB exists. Each model runs over the area you choose, on the terrain and geology already on the globe, and states its method, assumptions and sources beside the map. The methods are written up in the Journal so they can be checked and cited.

Landslides & slope stability

Sixteen days of hourly NOAA GFS rainfall, a water balance over the real drainage, and an infinite-slope Factor of Safety per cell from DEM slope and the strength of the mapped geology — a stack of rasters the clock plays through. Rockfall alongside. Method

Flood inundation

River flood extent by scenario and by discharge, spread over the streamed terrain and the mapped channels, with the same forecast rainfall driving the channel model. Method

Tropical cyclone risk

How often a cyclone passes within 200 km of any point, from every best-track record since 1842 — the IBTrACS archive that also plays on the free globe.

Seismic risk

Damaging shaking by magnitude, from the merged USGS ComCat, ISC-GEM and GEM GHEC record — 312,500 events, one per-magnitude map each. Method

Volcanic ashfall

Ashfall of at least a millimetre, by eruption size, from every volcano in the Smithsonian Holocene record — over the completeness window and over the whole record. Method

Sea level

Where the sea stands at a level you choose, spread from the real coastline through the streamed heights rather than by a flat threshold on elevation.

Membership also opens rock properties — strength, permeability and the rest of the geotechnical database mapped onto the world's geology — the Model Builder, which samples the real ground into a domain and packages the mesh for a solver, Earth Engine over any area you draw, and saving and exporting projects to your own disk. These are screening-level research tools with their methods stated; none is a certified warning service, and the disclaimer applies.

Our Vision

Democratizing Geohazard Science

Critical hazard data — accessible, portable, and globally distributed.

Open by construction, not by policy

Exposure to geohazards is rising; access to risk intelligence is not. People still depend on black-box assessments, delayed alerts and fragmented systems — and on being told what the ground under them is worth.

GeoHUB is the answer we can actually build: the catalogues on a free globe anyone can spin, the methods behind every model published in the Journal, and the models themselves sold for the price of a textbook — so a hazard map can be challenged rather than simply received.

Roadmap

What Comes Next

The models above run in your browser over the area you choose. The next step moves them onto hosted compute so they run continuously — and that is the one part of GeoID that still needs partners and infrastructure. The roadmap and partnership routes →

Next

Continuous forecasting, hosted

A GFS-to-Factor-of-Safety pipeline that runs every forecast cycle on a server rather than on demand in a tab, with saved locations and alerts. Needs always-on hosting and HPC or GPU time for the finite-element runs.

Validation

Region-by-region checks

Every hazard model tested against landslide inventories, flood records and historical events wherever the data is open, and written up in the Journal as each check is done — the bar a layer has to clear before it is called operational.
