Purpose

What this is

The prototype exists to answer one question about the toolset: can somebody produce a defensible regional hazard map with it, from public data, without leaving the application? Everything below was made with the tools the GIS page offers — slope, reclassify, rasterize, interpolate, distance-to-features, weighted overlay, sample-at-points — over five open datasets.

Both maps are susceptibility screening products: they rank ground by how prone it is to failure or inundation. They are not hazard maps and carry no return period. Operational flood mapping for Northern Ireland is hydraulic modelling by DfI Rivers under the EU Floods Directive.
ElevationCopernicus GLO-30 DSM (ESA/Airbus), warped to the working grid. Validated against two published heights: Slieve Donard 835.6 m against 850, Lough Neagh surface 10.5 m against 12.5.

Results

The maps

Transparent areas are genuinely unscored: the sea, and the Republic of Ireland, where the British Geological Survey's mapping stops. A cell is scored only where every factor has a value — a missing factor defaulting to zero would read as “safest” exactly where the data is worst.

Evidence

How it was validated

The test is the standard one: does the worst 30 % of the ground hold at least 70 % of the landslides that have actually happened?

It could not be tested on Northern Ireland. The BGS National Landslide Database returns a single record inside the Northern Ireland box, because that database covers Great Britain; the Northern Ireland inventory belongs to GSNI, which publishes no open service. A pass rate computed from one landslide would be meaningless, so the validator refuses any verdict below thirty.

The method was therefore validated where the inventory is dense — South Wales, 1,242 inventoried landslides, comparable geology of Carboniferous mudstones and steep valley sides, run through the identical recipe. It passes: 74.2 % of those landslides fall in classes covering 27.5 % of the ground, and the success-rate curve gives an AUC of 0.841.

That validation also found two genuine faults in the first version of the model, both corrected and re-measured rather than argued about: equal-interval class breaks cannot satisfy both halves of the criterion at once, and a weighted sum does not know that a landslide needs a slope — flat ground on weak rock beside a river was scoring like a scarp. Adding the slope condition moved the AUC from 0.826 to 0.841.

Reproduce it

Open it yourself

The prototype is a real project folder, and the maps carry their full provenance — which tool made them, with which parameters, from which inputs. To open it in myGeoID with the layers live on the globe:

  1. Run python3 serve.py in the repository, and open the localhost address it prints.
  2. Paste the sidecar's connect line into Research ▸ Settings ▸ Sidecar. The launcher prints it at startup.
  3. Open the earth/ni-prototype project on the Projects page. Its five source layers drape onto the globe, and the finished maps sit in the Data Repository under data/processed/.

The method, the reclassification tables and every measurement quoted here are written up in the repository at GeoID_GIS/docs/ni-prototype/, alongside a rerunnable script that reproduces both maps from the raw downloads.
