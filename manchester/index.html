    The land
    Old Trafford

    Every new street and bridge is named after a United icon: the Busby Babes by the Munich Memorial garden, the 1968 side on the Freightliner land, the Treble at the arena, Ferguson’s later sides on the north bank. Pick one to see it on the street map.

    The name

    “The Flowers of Manchester” is the lament written after the Munich air disaster of 6 February 1958, for the Busby Babes who did not come home. The arena carries its name so that the club’s new home starts from the memory of the team that made it.

    The rose

    The building is a flower laid on the city. A single skin of milky ETFE wraps the bowl: aqua at the base, the colour of the Old Trafford windows, warming to white at the crown. Two families of spirals, 13 one way and 21 the other, the Fibonacci numbers of a real flower head, cross to make the netting. Over them, 15 petals leave the roof opening, each turned 137.5°, the golden angle at which a rose sets its petals, and run down to the ground. The wall leans out 20° and turns over a curved eave into a roof that falls to the opening, so the whole arena reads as one bloom. The crest and the club’s name are set into the skin, and the crest is inlaid in the arrival square.

    The arena

    100,020 seats in three tiers on the 25-acre Indurent site at Trafford Wharf, its centre spot about 840 m from Old Trafford’s. Between the skin and the bowl is an eight-storey atrium: shops, restaurants and bars on stepped galleries, the museum, trophy room and Munich Memorial Gallery, the lounges and a sky restaurant. It works as an arcade every day of the week and as the concourse on matchdays.

    Old Trafford, kept

    Old Trafford is not demolished. Its upper tiers come off every stand, leaving one even tier of about 35,000 seats under a roof in the style of today’s South Stand: the home of the women’s team and the academy. The pitch, the tunnel, the Munich clock and memorial and the Holy Trinity statue stay where they are, and Legends’ Field sits behind the North Stand. The club keeps its two grounds, a short walk apart.

    The Sir Matt Busby Complex

    Around the two grounds, a new neighbourhood is named for the manager who built the Babes and rebuilt the club after Munich. Shops, bars, the galleria and the cinema cluster in the Matchday Quarter at the arena’s door, then give way to streets of courtyard homes on the Freightliner land, the north bank and the canal side, with hotels and bars by the East Stand.

    Arrival and the walks

    A new station on the Liverpool–Manchester line has two 230 m island platforms for 12-car matchday trains. From its square, the Busby Way, a 22 m fan walk with its own canal bridge, runs to the arena’s south gate and the Crest Plaza; the Trinity Walk leads to Old Trafford; and the Avenue of Legends runs along the canal between the two grounds.

    The garden and the Trinity Light

    At the heart of the neighbourhood, a botanic garden with a palm house holds the Munich Memorial: 23 red rose beds, one for each of the 23 lives lost. In it, exactly halfway between the two centre spots, stands the Trinity Light. Every evening it sends three beams into the sky, white, blue and green, for Bobby Charlton, Denis Law and George Best, the trinity of the 1968 European Cup side whose statue stands outside Old Trafford.

    The street names

    Every new street, bridge, lane, courtyard, arcade and yard is named after someone from the club’s story, district by district: the Busby Babes and Munich around the memorial garden, the 1968 side on the Freightliner land, the Treble at the arena, the pioneers by Old Trafford, and Ferguson’s later sides on the north bank, with Ella Toone for the women’s team. Bridges carry Eric Cantona and Bryan Robson.

    The wider world

    The scheme sits in Greater Manchester as the GeoHUB flight sim draws it: real buildings from OpenStreetMap, imagery out to the Pennines on real terrain, and at night NASA’s Black Marble city lights with lamps along every road.
