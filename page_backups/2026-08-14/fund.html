Research & Funding Prospectus

Interactive geoscience platforms are live. Real-time flood and landslide forecasting applications are now just one computational step away.

GeoID has built and deployed two accessible geoscience tools used by members today. The demos establish what the platform can do. The immediate next goal — driving physics-based hydrological models from live rainfall forecasts to produce a public flood and landslide risk index — is technically defined. What it needs is servers and HPC access.

STATUS: The Explorer platforms are live and usable today but the myGeoID flood and landslide forecasting system is the subject of this funding case, not a live service, the myGeoID prototype is only meant as a supplementary demo of the overall vision for the user interface.

What exists today

Two live platforms that demonstrate the approach

The Explorer viewers are not prototypes. They are working, publicly accessible tools built from real scientific datasets. Their purpose here is to show what accessible, interactive geoscience looks like — and that GeoID can build and sustain it.

Live today

Planet Explorer

Ten interactive viewers spanning every planet in the Solar System. Real topographic data, scientific overlays, guided tours. A working membership, authentication, and billing stack underpins it. Proof of sustained delivery — from raw planetary datasets to a polished public tool.

Live today

Earth Explorer — Etna

A 3D geological viewer built from published INGV data: seismicity catalogue (2011–2023), fault planes, geological map, magma plumbing architecture, historical lava flows, and an interactive cross-section. The closest thing to a research GIS that runs in a browser, free of institutional access requirements.

Ingested

Hazard data pipelines

Working ingestion of NOAA GFS global forecast data, IMERG precipitation, RainViewer radar, GEBCO bathymetry, and geological shapefiles. The raw material for an operational system is already flowing — it is not yet served continuously.

The viewers show one thing above all: that rigorous, interactive geoscience can be made genuinely accessible. A curious student and a working scientist use the same tool and both get something useful from it. That is the design principle GeoID applies to everything it builds, including the forecasting system.

The immediate goal

How does land respond to forecasted rainfall?

NOAA GFS provides global rainfall forecasts at 0.25° resolution, updated every six hours. The question GeoID is equipped to answer: given that forecast, how will a given hillslope or drainage catchment respond?

The answer comes from a finite-element hydrological model — the GALES multi-physics solver lineage applied to shallow slope stability and flood routing. The output is a Factor of Safety: a single, interpretable value that indicates whether a slope or catchment is in a stable, marginal, or failing condition, computed before the rainfall arrives.

This is not a new idea — the science is established in the literature. What does not exist is a public, continuously running, free implementation of it. That is what GeoID is building first.

Constraint 1 — Servers

Continuous GFS ingestion, time-series storage, and a public-facing web interface require always-on infrastructure. The data pipeline exists and has been tested. The hosting does not yet exist in a persistent form.

Constraint 2 — Computational Capacity

FEM slope-stability and flood-routing runs are computationally demanding. Running them continuously, at useful spatial resolution, across a pilot domain requires HPC allocation or dedicated cluster time. This is the principal cost this prospectus addresses.

The plan

Phases of development

Phase 0 — Platform and capability proof

Complete · Live

Build and ship accessible geoscience tools that demonstrate the platform can handle real scientific data at production quality.

  • Planet Explorer: ten planetary viewers with scientific overlays, tours, and membership billing
  • Earth Explorer — Etna: 3D geological viewer from published INGV datasets
  • Hazard data ingestion: NOAA GFS, IMERG, radar, GEBCO, shapefiles

Infrastructure: static hosting and client-side rendering — already covered.

Phase 1 — Live GFS ingestion and first hydrological model

Seeking funding

Stand up persistent infrastructure and run the first continuous GFS → FEM → Factor of Safety pipeline for a defined pilot domain.

  • Always-on server for scheduled GFS ingestion, storage, and data serving
  • FEM slope-stability and shallow flood-routing model driven by live GFS forecasts
  • Public-facing Factor of Safety output for the pilot domain
  • Validation against historical rainfall events and published landslide catalogues

Infrastructure: managed server (ingestion and web interface) plus HPC or GPU allocation for FEM runs. This is the core funding ask.

Phase 2 — Scale, public interface, and regional expansion

Seeking funding

Deliver the public-facing myGeoID experience and expand coverage beyond the pilot domain.

  • Personalised risk dashboards with saved locations and alert thresholds (myGeoID)
  • Additional pilot regions based on partner and funder priorities
  • Open data-access layer and reproducible model outputs for researchers
  • Longer-term: further hazard types as the FEM lineage is extended

Infrastructure: scaled hosting and storage as user base and regional coverage grow.

How to get involved

Engagement models

GeoID is a non-exclusive, open-science initiative. The most direct forms of support are HPC access and funded research partnerships, but we are open to all routes below.

High Performance Computing (HPC) & cloud in-kind

HPC allocations or cloud-credit programmes that directly cover the Phase 1 FEM workloads — the single most direct path to making the first forecast pipeline operational.

Research grant co-application

Join as a named partner or host institution on hazard, climate-resilience, or open-science funding calls. GeoID supplies the platform, modelling lineage, and proof of capability.

University & data partnership

Provide HPC time, validation datasets, or supervisory capacity in exchange for a live testbed for student and postdoctoral research and open teaching resources.

Institutional sponsorship

Civil-protection agencies, NGOs, or foundations seeking public-benefit early-warning capability in underserved regions, beginning with the Phase 1 pilot domain.

The annual Explorer membership (£19.99/year) demonstrates public demand for accessible geoscience tools. At early-stage scale it is not, and is not presented as, the financing mechanism for the forecasting platform — that is what this prospectus addresses.

Leadership

Who is building this

Owen McCluskey

Founder & Principal Investigator

Computational geoscientist (PhD, University of Pisa / INGV) specialising in finite-element modelling of geomechanical and hydrological systems using the GALES multi-physics solver, with research spanning magma dynamics at Mount Etna, glaciology, and volcanogenic flood modelling. The Etna viewer reflects direct domain expertise; the hydrological forecasting pipeline extends the same FEM approach toward public-facing hazard intelligence.

The demos are live and verifiable. The next step needs greater computational power.

We would welcome a conversation with any institution, funder, or research partner who wants to see physics-based flood and landslide forecasting become a free public resource.
